= Reverse interviews
Kieran Potts

Let's be honest: working in software is a _variable_ experience. Working at a software house as part of an in-house team feels very different from working at a consultancy serving a non-IT domain. A small startup feels very different from a large global firm. And even within each of those sub-sectors, the culture and ways of working vary enormously.

When you're applying for work in software, _extrinsic_ factors — things like salary and benefits, the tech stack, the problem space, the office and the work environment — will be the early filters for your job funnel. But what will matter more in the long term is the organization's culture and values — what it is actually like to work there day-to-day. _Intrinsic_ factors — purpose, autonomy, mastery — will determine how long you stay.

How do you discover these factors before you take up new employment? Unless you have inside knowledge of the organization you're applying to, you'll need to ask questions in your interviews to dig into this.

This is the "`reverse interview`" – questions the _candidate_ asks of their prospective employer before accepting a job offer. The trick is to design a series of questions – not too many – that will surface both red and green flags.

Juniors shouldn't be overly picky. All experience is good at the start of a career. Even if you land in a sweaty dev shop, you will at least learn how _not_ to make software! But as you progress through your career, those intrinsic factors — and the _quality_ of the work — will matter more and more to you.

For the rest of us, we should be brave and ask straight questions. Good employers will respond well to it, while bad employers will get defensive, even offended — your first red flag.

== Pre-filtering

You can do a lot of filtering before you even apply. Use websites like Glassdoor and your own network. Don't be afraid to reach out to _former_ employees on LinkedIn and ask why they left, and what they liked and disliked. (Don't do this with _current_ employees — it is risky for them, and pride gets in the way of honesty.)

// Do your own background checks, too. The tech industry is a small world; use your network. Glassdoor is a good barometer, but look for _patterns_ rather than individual reviews — be wary of companies with lots of 5-star and lots of 1-star reviews; most companies are, by definition, average, and the most honest picture tends to be lots of 3- and 4-star reviews.

Do some background checks on the organization's existing software developers, without connecting to them directly. Find them on LinkedIn, GitHub, and the socials. Do any speak at conferences or keep a blog? Does the organization itself have an engineering blog, contribute to open source projects, or publish technical standards, etc.?

Red flags can emerge from the job spec itself, before you have any contact. Consider its wording, emphasis, and tone. "`Fast-paced`" and "`no two days the same`" may be code for "`chaotic`" and "`no time to do things properly`". "`Quality-focused`" and "`learning-oriented`" are strong hints that the culture and values are aligned in the opposite direction.

Be wary of a spec that checks off a laundry list of commercial experience with specific languages and libraries. That suggests a tactical, not strategic, approach to recruitment. Such short-termism leads to high churn.

Be wary of multi-stage processes. Two or three stages is fine; four or five is _probably_ a red flag — unless the role is strategically important for the organization.

Be wary, too, of requests for free work. Take-home technical tests are fine, but contributions to actual projects should be paid.

== Early feels

You'll start to get a feel for an organization's culture and values through the screening and subsequent interview rounds. Look out for these red flags:

* Negative or toxic vibes from the interviewers.

* Weird power dynamics. A disrespectful interview that tries to throw you off or intimidate you is a glimpse of the culture to come.

* An interviewer who gets offended or defensive at a legitimate question. Your career affects your life; you have the right to ask hard questions.

* Interviewers who sound exhausted and fed up.

* Disengaged interviewers who check their phones or don't remember your answers. You deserve their full attention.

* Lots of buzzwords, sometimes used incorrectly.

* An interview that follows a checklist of questions, with no deviation or follow-up based on your answers – the whole thing's just ticking boxes.

* Technical questions that are at an inappropriate level of abstraction for the job role. For example, "`explain how you'd protect against SQL injection`" may be appropriate if you'll be expected to write lots of custom low-level library code, but is less useful to judge if someone will be able to solve problems at the levels of applications and systems.

* A suspiciously high salary for the title, or unusual benefits like unlimited holiday. This is a sure sign that the organization will justify stress, and perhaps toxicity, with above-market remuneration. Don't tell yourself "`it's just a stepping stone`".

* Or the opposite — below average pay — and promises of a higher salary "`later`". And organization that can't pay market rates now probably never will be able to.

* "`We're a family.`" Sometimes this is a genuine attempt to create effective, collaborate cultures, but too often it's a way of getting you to work longer hours, or for less pay, so as not to "`let the family down`".

* Questions like "`do you work well under pressure?`" or "`tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline`". These are good questions to turn around — reply by asking a clarifying question like "`what does high pressure look like in this role?`", and "`where does that pressure come from?`" Pressure usually comes from one or two sources: (a) the pressure is fabricated through artificial agency, the result of poor management and the psychological danger of replying "`no`"; (b) constant production incidents and firefighting, which means the organization is not investing in automation and non-functional qualities.

Right through to the point of an offer being made, keep looking out for those red flags. Be alert to pressure to accept an offer quickly — it's a sure sign they're desperate, which means they're either under-staffed or projects have gone wildly off course. And watch out for the bait-and-switch, where you apply for a specific role at a specific salary, but the role is switched at the last minute for something lower.

// * _If I started tomorrow, what would I be working on?_ Beware the bait-and-switch where you are sold a dream and end up on something else (usually: legacy). It is not always answerable — you may be hired into a "`talent pool`" — but you should expect honesty, even "`we don't know yet`".

// If the interviewers are hazy about the tech stack, the role, or progression, you may be looking at snap hiring — a panic to fill a vacancy after someone left. That suggests under-resourcing and poor resource planning.

== The basics

The reverse interview itself — which you should request, if no time is given in the formal interview rounds — is a chance to nail down the basics of the job, things like:

* IT provision (computer, monitors, ergonomics).
* Core/flexible/office hours.
* Overtime policy (and how overtime is compensated).
* Remote and hybrid policy.
* Holiday allowance.
* Sick leave and parental policies.
* Health and wellness benefits.
* Pension contributions.
* Learning budgets/time.

// What's your policy on working hours? What time do people sign off Slack — are evenings and weekends ever expected?

So far, so checkboxy. But from this baseline of understanding you want to drill down into exactly what _success looks like_ in the role. Here are some questions to help interrogate this:

* "`Give me an example of someone in a role like mine who did really well here. What did they do to succeed?`"
* "`What are the biggest challenges currently facing the team?`"
* "`How do you monitor and measure individual performance?`"
* "`How do you evaluate individuals versus the whole team?`"

On hours: "`How many hours are you contracted for, and how many do people work in practice?`" Beware answers like "`we don't really have fixed hours, you just work as you need to to get the job done`" — that usually means long hours, no overtime, and deadlines set by the business rather than the team.

You're applying not for a job for the next 12 months, but (hopefully) for a long and fruitful career. Look ahead. Ask questions like: "`When do performance and pay reviews come around? How are they done? Is pay adjusted for inflation? How does the company support professional growth and development? What is the policy on internal hiring and promotions? How are promotions handled?`" These sorts of questions reveal how much the organization _really_ values its people. Fair and transparent systems of pay review and promotion are a good sign; secretive processes are a massive red flag.

// Look for a mix of individual and team evaluation, with a slight emphasis on the team; for _value added_, not "`doing whatever it takes to meet deadlines`". Listen for rewarding firefighting and long hours — long hours as a badge of honor is a red flag. If the interviewer's answers about success are vague, politely ask for clarification.

If you're really brave, ask: "`What is the average tenure of a developer here?`" Incredibly, the average tenure for a permanent tech worker is only about two years. But some organizations buck the trend, and they'll happily tell you why.

If you'll be on-site, consider the physical environment. Is the office a nice place to work? Are there secluded spaces where you can go to _think_? Will the space be hot in summer, cold in winter? These things are more important that free tea and biscuits.

== Topics

Structure your reverse interview around three to five core topics that you want to explore. The focus will vary depending on the nature of your role and your level of seniority.

Below, I've listed example questions under seven possible topics:

* The product
* Requirements specification
* Planning (including prioritization and estimation)
* Architecture and quality
* The software development lifecycle
* Ways of working

=== The product

* "`How are your products differentiated from competitors'?`"

* For startups: "`What problem are you solving? Have you achieved product/market fit, or are you still pivoting? How do you measure the value new features give users?`" Avoid solutions looking for a problem.

* "`How is the business funded? What's the turnover and profit generated from the product? What's the growth trajectory?`" Even private companies should disclose at least a ballpark turnover figure. If the company will not disclose this, ask why not. There may be legitimate reasons for not sharing sensitive financials, but employees deserve some transparency into their employer's financial health. If there's no revenue yet (eg. it's a startup), ask: "`How much runway do you have?`"

* "`What are the goals for long-term product development?`" And follow-up: "`If you achieve those goals, what will the reward look like for the staff?`"

* Ask directly: "`Do you plan to sell the company?`" A company built to be sold has a very different culture from one built to last — more short-term and tactical, with fewer opportunities for progression or investment in the long-term health of the software.

=== Requirements specification

* "`Where do functional requirements come from?`" Look for the business to define high-level acceptance criteria, not to design the solution through user stories.

* "`How are requirements specified?`" They should be written from a user's perspective. If they are just task descriptions, suspect a feature factory.

* "`How are cross-functional requirements, like performance and security, specified?`"

* "`How do you validate requirements?`" This question isn't about testing that the built software meets its requirements. It's about validating that the requirements are genuine — they represent real problems that real users need solutions to. Requirements should be driven by users' needs.

=== Planning

* "`How do you plan and prioritize work? Who decides the sequencing, and who works on what?`"

* "`Are your requirements stable or volatile, and how do you manage that volatility? How do you manage change requests (scope creep)?`"

* "`How is technical debt and refactoring prioritized against feature delivery? How is time for it factored into the process?`"

* "`How do you ensure delivery is sustainable and keeps costs low?`"

* "``"

* "`How do you estimate work? When do you estimate — T-shirt sizes from requirements, time estimates after a prototype or some initial implementation?`"

* "`What do you do if the estimates don't align with the organization's targets? What happens if something is delivered later than estimated?`" For example: how would they handle a developer who gave a high-level estimate but then found the optimal solution was twice as complex as anticipated?

What you are listening for is whether the organization prefers _predictability_ or _efficiency_. Predictability ("`it gets done when we said it would`") requires a heavyweight process with lots of up-front design and resource planning. Optimizing for efficiency allows a more lightweight process and good modern practices. If they front-load design, probe why: big design up front can succeed, but it can also signal command-and-control and a lack of trust.

The only guaranteed way to deliver predictably is to do _less_. That means prioritizing ruthlessly and delivering the most valuable features first. Red flags include answers about adding more people or spending more time justifying the estimates. Look for organizations that understand the distinction between an _estimate_, a _target_, and a _commitment_ — and that estimates get more accurate the further into the life cycle you are, so a commitment comes only once the _design_ is understood, not just the requirements.

You are interested in how they balance the immediate needs of the business against the long-term health of the software and the team — strategic, long-term thinking over tactical, short-term fixes. Look for a product backlog kept separate from the sprint backlog, and a balance between delivering value and maintaining the software's health. We do two kinds of work: developing and operating the functional and non-functional requirements, and investing in the quality of the software and our ways of working. Product owners should _not_ have full control over prioritization. You are trying to learn whether they see engineering as a cost center — a higher chance you'll be miserable. Micromanaged teams and fixed deadlines are red flags.

* "`Have you ever had a project take two to four times longer than planned? What happened, and what did you learn?`"

=== Architecture and quality

* "`Could you describe the high-level architecture of the system, conceptually?`" The answer is revealing. Systems mirror the organizations that build them, so a tightly-coupled distributed monolith suggests a highly centralized, top-down organization.

* "`Has the system maintained good conceptual integrity over time, even as the architecture has evolved?`" If the architecture has good conceptual integrity, the team should be able to describe it without discussing implementation details — no mention of the technology stack or infrastructure. Something like:

[quote]
----
An API gateway serves web and mobile clients. The gateway routes requests to a small number of large domain services. Each domain service manages its own data persistence, and services share data primarily using asynchronous messages. The domain services are supported by a large number of stateless microservices, which they communicate with synchronously via RPC-like calls.
----

* "`If there was one thing about how the software is designed and made that you could change, what would it be?`"

* "`How do you communicate design ideas?`"

* "`Who makes design decisions? How do you choose between options?`"

=== Ways of working

Look for an iterative, incremental process driven by users' needs, a mix of synchronous and asynchronous communication, and good use of documentation and RFCs to reduce communication overhead. Limited opportunities for remote and async working is a red flag in the post-Covid era — often a sign of low trust. There can be legitimate reasons for on-site work, but if they cite "`collaboration`", drill into what you would actually do on office days; if it is all meetings, those can usually be done remotely.

Also watch for a highly siloed organization. You want teams making their own choices _and_ collaborating across teams — cross-functional but not siloed, with everyone shifting left so that testers help define acceptance criteria and developers help define the scope of stories.

// Flexibility and work-life balance are not just perks; they are essential for sustained satisfaction and productivity. Ultimately we are assessing the _quality_ of the software systems, because that has the greatest impact on the developer experience — on avoiding burnout, working sustainably, and being able to build a long-term career.

* "`Can you tell me about the team I'll be working with?`"

* Questions that explore the boundaries of responsibilities between product and developers.

* "`How do you manage change in software?`" Open-ended questions like this reveal what the organization thinks matters most.

* "`How long does it take to get a working dev environment up and running?`"

* "`When is a task "ready" for development?`"

* "`Do you refactor as you go along?`"

* "`What's your approach to code review?`" Look for collective code ownership.

* "`Do you pair or mob? Under what circumstances?`"

// _Do you do pair programming?_ It's not necessarily a red flag if they don't, but dismissing it out of hand suggests a feature factory. Reasonable reasons why it didn't work for them are neither a red nor a green flag.

// _How do you support asynchronous working?_ Investment here supports work-life balance and sustainable working; its absence can signal command-and-control and low autonomy.

* "`How long do feature branches stay open? Is anyone allowed to commit directly to the mainline?`"

* Opening question: "`What is the team's dynamic and work style?`"

* "`What is your onboarding process?`" No formal process is a red flag; an overly long one suggests heavy bureaucracy.

* "`What does a typical workday look like? Describe your development methodology. What's the level of autonomy?`"

* "`What ceremonies do you do — stand-ups, retrospectives? What happens if you don't complete the work you committed to in a sprint?`"

* "`How do you ensure a sustainable pace?`"

* "`How do you communicate?`"

* "`How can I contribute ideas to improve the ways of working?`"

* "`How do you balance collaboration with the need for deep focus?`" Distinguish genuine teamwork (extended focus solving a problem together) from "`collaboration`" that just means being on hand to answer queries at a moment's notice.

For autonomy, look for signals that teams make their own choices:

* "`Is your culture one of individual or collective responsibility?`"

* "`How do you set objectives and targets?`" Look for team-level measures, not individual ones.

* "`Who is responsible for the design, test, and quality of code? If a feature needs the code reshaped, who decides?`" If it's the product owner or architects, suspect top-down, remote-control programming.

=== Testing

* "`What's your test coverage? Do the tests evolve at the same time as the code?`"

* "`What levels of automated testing do you have — unit, integration, system — and which matter most for this solution?`"

* "`What do your acceptance tests look like, and how are they linked to the requirements specification?`"

* "`What other testing do you do — penetration, load, usability? How much manual testing before each release?`"

* "`Do you do test-driven development? How do you define "good" and "done"? When do you test?`" (Shift left as much as possible.)

=== Delivery automation

High-level of automation are a green flag.

_Deployment_ is installing and enabling the software in an environment; _release_ is making it available to users. Deployment is technical and can happen frequently (in CI/CD); releases are business-oriented and usually less frequent.

* "`What's your release cadence? What's the cycle time between a change being "done" and reaching production?`"

* "`Do you ship incomplete features behind feature flags?`"

* "`What steps prepare a release? What determines releasability? How often do you release?`"

* "`How many bugs do you have? If you find a problem in production, what steps are taken?`"

* "`What are your branching-and-merging strategies?`"

* "`How often do you ship to production? How often does a release cause problems in production?`" DORA metrics!

* "`What are your pain points? What's the biggest challenge facing delivery right now? What needs improving most?`"

* "`How is progress reported to the business?`"

* "`What was the last big software failure, and how did you deal with it?`"

=== Operations and maintenance

* "`How are production services operated? First-line support? Out-of-hours support?`"

* "`How quickly do you recover from production incidents? What's your monitoring and observability like? How are incidents managed?`"

* "`What keeps you up at night?`"

* "`What do your development environments look like?`" Poor development environments — lots of interdependencies, not isolated, not containerized — are a red flag.

* "`What happens if a bug ships to production? How do you approach post-mortems?`"

=== Culture and values

This is probably the most important consideration — it will determine whether you enjoy the job and how long you stay. Areas to probe include the IT department structure and team boundaries; practices (dailies, retrospectives, refinement, planning, 1:1s); async versus sync working and communication tools; working hours and flexibility; degree of autonomy; the quality of the working environment; guiding principles like psychological safety and blameless post-mortems; the volume of meetings; and focus versus context-switching.

Look for continuous learning, a blameless culture (psychological safety), investment in quality, collaboration with domain experts, and knowledge sharing rather than silos — in short, autonomy, mastery, and purpose. Look for _good_ busy, not endless meetings, interruptions, and context-switching. There is a misconception that noisy communication is collaboration; it isn't, it's just noise. Collaboration is working together to solve problems productively.

In a nutshell: you want me, and the team, to make software that meets your requirements and serves your users well, as cheaply as possible. I can do that — but a prerequisite is a healthy work-life balance, which is less about hours worked than about the culture, the autonomy over how we work, and the space to refine the craft we love. Vague answers about work-life balance are a bad sign.

What you are looking for is an organization — or at least an IT division — that understands the essence of software development at a fundamental level: that what we do is not _code_, but _think_. One of the biggest predictors of a team's success is the degree to which people feel safe to express ideas and contribute. The DORA research identifies _psychological safety_ as a key factor: individuals need a safe space to take risks and make mistakes without fear of recrimination.

Ours is a discipline primarily focused on _learning_. Whatever the sector, developing software is a process of continuous learning — not only that individuals grow, but that we make software to solve problems, and finding those solutions is a process of discovery, experimentation, and adjustment based on feedback. On a greenfield project you may start building without even knowing exactly what it is you are building. These are the real skills — not deep knowledge of any one technology, but the skills that let us learn new things.

* "`How would you describe the leadership style here?`" The reverse equivalent of "`What do you look for in a boss?`"

* "`What are the organization values? What does the organization value most from software delivery?`" "`Of your organization values, which do you and your team live by, and how?`" Many organizations publish values that are not embedded in their decisions. Real values frame every decision at every level; "`great place to work`" is easy to claim.

* "`How do you benefit from ensuring a good work-life balance? How do you ensure a low-stress, low-noise, low-friction environment?`"

* "`In the last 12 months, how many IT people left, as a percentage of the total at the start of the year?`"

== Making a decision

Gather all the data — Glassdoor, Trustpilot, Companies House — and then go with your gut. The few times in my career I have ignored my gut, it has been a disaster. If you don't get a good vibe from the people interviewing you, ask whether you really want to work with them: organizations attract and retain similar types of people, so the interviewer is probably representative. Do you think you can learn from them, work with them, and have fun doing so?

Remote and flexible working are not always possible — there can be legitimate reasons, such as the nature of the work or high security. If you have reservations but are willing to roll the dice, be honest and share them. And wait at least 24 hours before accepting an offer; pressure to accept quickly is a red flag.

////

Treat the interview as a two-way process. If there is no time in the main cycle, request a "`reverse interview`" once you have an offer — it shows diligence. Treat all of these as _leading_ questions that guide your approach and prompt you to explore tangents.

A note on red flags: you are really looking for a _combination_ of them. One on its own is not necessarily a problem, but a cluster is a sign the place is not a good one to work. Red flags turn up in green companies and vice versa, so consider the whole picture.
