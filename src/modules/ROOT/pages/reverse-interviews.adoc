= Reverse interviews
Kieran Potts
:description: An interview is a two-way process. Here is the set of "reverse interview" questions I use to assess the real quality of a software job — the red and green flags that reveal what a place is actually like to work.

Let's be honest: working in the software industry can be a _varied_ experience.

Working at a software house feels very different from working on an in-house team, or at a consultancy serving a non-IT domain. A small startup feels very different from a large global firm. And even within each sub-sector, the culture and ways of working vary enormously.

_Extrinsic_ factors — salary and benefits, the tech stack, the problem space, the office and the work environment — are the early filters for your job funnel. But what matters more in the long term is the organization's culture and values: what it is actually like to work there day to day. _Intrinsic_ factors — purpose, autonomy, mastery — determine how long you stay.

Juniors shouldn't be overly picky. All experience is good; even if you land in a sweaty dev shop, you will at least learn how _not_ to make software. Early in your career you may well be more motivated by the extrinsic factors. But as you progress, the intrinsic ones — and the quality of the work — matter more and more.

Unless you have inside knowledge of an organization, you will need to ask questions in your interviews to dig into this. I recently went through a round of interviews and put together a set of "`reverse interview`" questions, designed to surface the red and green flags that reveal a job's quality at a more subjective level.

Be brave, and ask straight questions. Don't be afraid to probe. Good employers respond well to it, appreciating honesty and openness; bad employers get defensive, even offended. Treat the interview as a two-way process. If there is no time in the main cycle, request a "`reverse interview`" once you have an offer — it shows diligence. Treat all of these as _leading_ questions that guide your approach and prompt you to explore tangents.

A note on red flags: you are really looking for a _combination_ of them. One on its own is not necessarily a problem, but a cluster is a sign the place is not a good one to work. Red flags turn up in green companies and vice versa, so consider the whole picture.

== Filtering before you apply

You can do a lot of filtering with Glassdoor and your own network. Don't be afraid to reach out to _former_ employees on LinkedIn and ask why they left, and what they liked and disliked. (Don't do this with _current_ employees — it is risky for them, and pride gets in the way of honesty.)

Red flags can emerge from the job spec itself, before you have any contact. Consider its wording, emphasis, and tone. "`Fast-paced`" and "`no two days the same`" may be code for "`chaotic`" and "`no time to do things properly`". "`Quality-focused`" and "`learning-oriented`" are code for caring about doing things properly. Be wary of a spec that checks off a laundry list of _commercial_ experience with specific languages and libraries — that suggests tactical recruitment.

Do some background checks on the company's other developers. Find them on LinkedIn, then look at their GitHub and social profiles. Do any speak at conferences or keep a blog? Does the company have a development blog, open-source projects, or published technical standards?

== Red flags in the interview process itself

Tech recruitment is broken — that's a whole other post — but be wary of multi-stage processes. Two or three stages is fine; four or five is probably a red flag, a sign the organization is bad at making decisions. Other red flags:

* A checklist Q&A format ("`explain how you'd protect against SQL injection`"). You should know this, but you probably haven't thought about it in years because the library abstracts it away. This tests recall, not your ability to solve problems and find answers.
* "`Drive to meet time, cost, and quality targets.`" You can fix one or two of these, not all three.
* A suspiciously high salary for the title. It probably means you won't be treated like a senior developer — that they will try to justify the stress with extra money. Don't tell yourself "`it's just a stepping stone`".
* Negative or toxic vibes from the interviewers, or interviewers who sound exhausted and fed up.
* Buzzwords, often used incorrectly.
* Lots of (or "`unlimited`") holiday, paired with inflated job titles.
* "`We're a family.`" No. Teams are fine; "`family`" is a way of getting you to work longer hours for less pay so as not to "`let the family down`".
* Promises of a higher salary "`later`".
* An interviewer who gets offended or defensive at a legitimate question. Your career affects your life; you have the right to ask hard questions.
* Weird power dynamics — a disrespectful interview that tries to throw you off or intimidate you is a glimpse of the culture to come.
* The bait-and-switch: you applied for a specific role at a specific salary, and now the role has "`changed`" and the salary is lower.
* Disengaged interviewers who check their phones or don't remember your answers. You deserve their full attention.
* Pressure to accept the offer quickly — a sign they are desperate, which means understaffing or projects gone off course.
* Requests for free work. Take-home tests are fine; contributions to actual projects should be paid.
* Full on-site work — do they truly understand software?

If an interviewer asks "`Do you work well under pressure?`", turn it around: "`What does high pressure look like in this role, and how is that pressure built?`" It will be one of three things:

. You walked into an interview for a nursing or firefighting job by mistake. Apologize and back away slowly.
. The pressure is fabricated through artificial urgency, the result of poor management and the psychological danger of saying no.
. The role deals with constant production incidents and firefighting, high turnover, and you will need to find a way to make it better.

They may not say which. Their answer will tell you everything you need to know.

== The basics

Reverse interviews are also a chance to nail down the basics: IT provision (computer, monitors, ergonomics); core/flexible/office hours and the overtime policy (and how overtime is compensated); remote and hybrid policy; holiday allowance; sick leave and parental policies; health and wellness benefits; pension contributions; and learning budgets or time. Is the office a nice place to work — are there secluded spaces where you can go to _think_?

On hours: "`How many hours are you contracted for, and how many do people work in practice?`" Beware answers like "`we don't really have fixed hours, you just work as you need to to get the job done`" — that usually means long hours, no overtime, and deadlines set by the business rather than the team.

Ask, too: _How are pay reviews handled? How is pay adjusted for inflation? How are promotions handled?_ This reveals how much the organization values its people. Fair and transparent is a good sign; secretive is a red flag. Of course it "`varies from person to person`", but it should happen within a framework you can understand.

=== Career advancement

* _What does success look like in this role?_
* _What are the biggest challenges currently facing the team?_
* _How does the company support professional growth and development?_
* _When do performance and pay reviews come around, and what's the process? Are there pay bands or other forms of pay transparency?_
* _What is the policy on internal hiring and promotions?_
* _What are your key performance indicators? How do you monitor and measure individual performance? How do you evaluate individuals versus the whole team? How do you measure quality? Do you measure progress against estimates?_
* _Give me an example of someone in a role like mine who did really well here. What did they do to succeed?_
* _How do you promote learning?_ Look not just for budgets and time for conferences and courses, but a culture where learning on the job is valued. Are they hiring for what you already know, or for your potential to learn? Will you have a coach or mentor?
* _What is the average tenure of a developer here?_ A low figure is a red flag. Average tenure in tech is around 18 months (yes, really), but some teams buck the trend, and they will happily explain why.

Look for a mix of individual and team evaluation, with a slight emphasis on the team; for _value added_, not "`doing whatever it takes to meet deadlines`". Listen for rewarding firefighting and long hours — long hours as a badge of honour is a red flag. If the interviewer's answers about success are vague, politely ask for clarification.

== Product

* _How are your products differentiated from competitors'?_ Understand what the organization does beforehand; this should reveal more than the website does.
* _What's your turnover and profit, and how has it changed year on year?_ Even private companies should disclose turnover. There may be genuine reasons not to share sensitive data, but employees deserve some transparency into the organization's financial health.
* _If I started tomorrow, what would I be working on?_ Beware the bait-and-switch where you are sold a dream and end up on something else (usually: legacy). It is not always answerable — you may be hired into a "`talent pool`" — but you should expect honesty, even "`we don't know yet`".
* _What are the organization's goals? If you achieve them, what will it look like?_

For startups, add: _What problem are you solving? Have you achieved product/market fit, or are you still pivoting? How do you measure the value new features give users? What's your revenue, and the trend?_ And if there is no revenue yet: _How is the business funded? How much runway do you have?_ Little runway and no product/market fit usually means a challenging culture. Avoid solutions looking for a problem.

Finally, ask directly: _Do you plan to sell the company?_ A company built to be sold has a very different culture from one built to last — more short-term and tactical, with fewer opportunities for progression or investment in the long-term health of the software.

== Requirements

This next group interrogates how the organization manages changes through their life-cycle phases.

* _Where do functional requirements come from? How are they specified? How are cross-functional requirements, like performance and security, specified? How do you verify requirements?_
* _How do you define requirements?_ They should be written from a user's perspective. If they are just task descriptions, suspect a feature factory.

Look for the business to define high-level acceptance criteria, not to design the solution through user stories; for feedback loops and continuous evaluation; and for requirements driven by users' needs.

== Design

* _Could you describe the high-level architecture of the system, conceptually?_ The answer is revealing. Systems mirror the organizations that build them, so a tightly-coupled distributed monolith suggests a highly centralized, top-down organization.
* _Has the system maintained good conceptual integrity over time, even as the architecture has evolved?_
* _If there was one thing about how the software is designed and made that you could change, what would it be?_
* _Who makes design decisions? How do you communicate design ideas, and choose between options?_

If the architecture has good conceptual integrity, the team should be able to describe it _without_ discussing implementation details — something like:

[quote]
____
An API gateway serves web and mobile clients. The gateway routes requests to a small number of large domain services. Each domain service manages its own data persistence, and services share data primarily using asynchronous messages. The domain services are supported by a large number of stateless microservices, which they communicate with synchronously via RPC-like calls.
____

This makes no mention of the technology stack or infrastructure. Describing the architecture in terms of the _business domain_ is a positive sign — it suggests the architecture is driven by the business, not the whims of developers. Unmaintainable legacy software is a red flag.

== Estimates

* _How do you estimate work? When do you estimate — T-shirt sizes from requirements, time estimates after a prototype or some initial implementation?_
* _What do you do if the estimates don't align with the organization's targets? What happens if something is delivered later than estimated?_ For example: how would they handle a developer who gave a high-level estimate but then found the optimal solution was twice as complex as anticipated?

What you are listening for is whether the company prefers _predictability_ or _efficiency_. Predictability ("`it gets done when we said it would`") requires a heavyweight process with lots of up-front design and resource planning. Optimizing for efficiency allows a more lightweight process and good modern practices. If they front-load design, probe why: big design up front can succeed, but it can also signal command-and-control and a lack of trust.

The only guaranteed way to deliver predictably is to do _less_. That means prioritizing ruthlessly and delivering the most valuable features first. Red flags include answers about adding more people or spending more time justifying the estimates. Look for organizations that understand the distinction between an _estimate_, a _target_, and a _commitment_ — and that estimates get more accurate the further into the life cycle you are, so a commitment comes only once the _design_ is understood, not just the requirements.

== Prioritization and planning

* _How do you plan and prioritize work? Who decides the sequencing, and who works on what?_
* _Are your requirements stable or volatile, and how do you manage that volatility? How do you manage change requests (scope creep)?_
* _How is technical debt and refactoring prioritized against feature delivery? How is time for it factored into the process?_
* _How do you ensure delivery is sustainable and keeps costs low?_
* _How do you manage change in software?_ Open-ended questions like this reveal what the organization thinks matters most.

You are interested in how they balance the immediate needs of the business against the long-term health of the software and the team — strategic, long-term thinking over tactical, short-term fixes. Look for a product backlog kept separate from the sprint backlog, and a balance between delivering value and maintaining the software's health. We do two kinds of work: developing and operating the functional and non-functional requirements, and investing in the quality of the software and our ways of working. Product owners should _not_ have full control over prioritization. You are trying to learn whether they see engineering as a cost centre — a higher chance you'll be miserable. Micromanaged teams and fixed deadlines are red flags.

== Construction

* _How long does it take to get a working dev environment up and running?_
* _When is a task "ready" for development?_
* _Do you refactor as you go along?_
* _What's your approach to code review?_ (Look for collective code ownership.)
* _Do you pair or mob? Under what circumstances?_
* _How long do feature branches stay open? Is anyone allowed to commit directly to the mainline?_

== Testing

* _What's your test coverage? Do the tests evolve at the same time as the code?_
* _What levels of automated testing do you have — unit, integration, system — and which matter most for this solution?_
* _What do your acceptance tests look like, and how are they linked to the requirements specification?_
* _What other testing do you do — penetration, load, usability? How much manual testing before each release?_
* _Do you do test-driven development? How do you define "good" and "done"? When do you test?_ (Shift left as much as possible.)

"`Good`" is about testing and wider quality assurance; "`done`" is about the software being ready for release.

== Deployment and release

_Deployment_ is installing and enabling the software in an environment; _release_ is making it available to users. Deployment is technical and can happen frequently (in CI/CD); releases are business-oriented and usually less frequent.

* _What's your release cadence? What's the cycle time between a change being "done" and reaching production?_
* _Do you ship incomplete features behind feature flags?_
* _What steps prepare a release? What determines releasability? How often do you release?_
* _How many bugs do you have? If you find a problem in production, what steps are taken?_
* _What are your branching-and-merging strategies?_

In DORA terms: how often do you ship to production, and how often does a release cause problems? High levels of automation are a green flag.

== Operations and maintenance

* _How are production services operated? First-line support? Out-of-hours support?_
* _How quickly do you recover from production incidents? What's your monitoring and observability like? How are incidents managed?_
* _What keeps you up at night?_
* _What do your development environments look like?_

Poor development environments — lots of interdependencies, not isolated, not containerized — are a red flag.

== Teams and ways of working

Opening question: _What is the team's dynamic and work style?_ Then start with onboarding: _What is your onboarding process?_ No formal process is a red flag; an overly long one suggests heavy bureaucracy.

* _What does a typical workday look like? Describe your development methodology. What's the level of autonomy?_
* _What ceremonies do you do — stand-ups, retrospectives? What happens if you don't complete the work you committed to in a sprint?_
* _Have you ever had a project take two to four times longer than planned? What happened, and what did you learn?_
* _Can you tell me about the team I'll be working with? What happens if a bug ships to production? How do you approach post-mortems?_
* _How do you ensure a sustainable pace? How do you communicate? How can I contribute ideas to improve the ways of working?_
* _How do you balance collaboration with the need for deep focus?_ Distinguish genuine teamwork (extended focus solving a problem together) from "`collaboration`" that just means being on hand to answer queries at a moment's notice.
* _What are your pain points? What's the biggest challenge facing delivery right now? What needs improving most? How is progress reported to the business?_
* _What's your policy on working hours? What time do people sign off Slack — are evenings and weekends ever expected?_
* _Do you do pair programming?_ It's not necessarily a red flag if they don't, but dismissing it out of hand suggests a feature factory. Reasonable reasons why it didn't work for them are neither a red nor a green flag.
* _How do you support asynchronous working?_ Investment here supports work-life balance and sustainable working; its absence can signal command-and-control and low autonomy.

Look for an iterative, incremental process driven by users' needs, a mix of synchronous and asynchronous communication, and good use of documentation and RFCs to reduce communication overhead. Limited opportunities for remote and async working is a red flag in the post-Covid era — often a sign of low trust. There can be legitimate reasons for on-site work, but if they cite "`collaboration`", drill into what you would actually do on office days; if it is all meetings, those can usually be done remotely.

If the interviewers are hazy about the tech stack, the role, or progression, you may be looking at snap hiring — a panic to fill a vacancy after someone left. That suggests under-resourcing and poor resource planning.

For autonomy, look for signals that teams make their own choices:

* _Is your culture one of individual or collective responsibility?_
* _How do you set objectives and targets?_ (Look for team-level measures, not individual ones.)
* _Who is responsible for the design, test, and quality of code? If a feature needs the code reshaped, who decides?_ If it's the product owner or architects, suspect top-down, remote-control programming.
* _What was the last big software failure, and how did you deal with it?_

Also watch for a highly siloed organization. You want teams making their own choices _and_ collaborating across teams — cross-functional but not siloed, with everyone shifting left so that testers help define acceptance criteria and developers help define the scope of stories.

== Leadership, culture, and values

This is probably the most important consideration — it will determine whether you enjoy the job and how long you stay. Areas to probe include the IT department structure and team boundaries; practices (dailies, retrospectives, refinement, planning, 1:1s); async versus sync working and communication tools; working hours and flexibility; degree of autonomy; the quality of the working environment; guiding principles like psychological safety and blameless post-mortems; the volume of meetings; and focus versus context-switching.

* _How would you describe the leadership style here?_ (The reverse equivalent of "`What do you look for in a boss?`")
* _What are the company values? What does the organization value most from software delivery?_
* _Of your company values, which do you and your team live by, and how?_ Many organizations publish values that are not embedded in their decisions. Real values frame every decision at every level; "`great place to work`" is easy to claim.
* _How do you benefit from ensuring a good work-life balance? How do you ensure a low-stress, low-noise, low-friction environment?_
* _In the last 12 months, how many IT people left, as a percentage of the total at the start of the year?_

Look for continuous learning, a blameless culture (psychological safety), investment in quality, collaboration with domain experts, and knowledge sharing rather than silos — in short, autonomy, mastery, and purpose. Look for _good_ busy, not endless meetings, interruptions, and context-switching. There is a misconception that noisy communication is collaboration; it isn't, it's just noise. Collaboration is working together to solve problems productively.

In a nutshell: you want me, and the team, to make software that meets your requirements and serves your users well, as cheaply as possible. I can do that — but a prerequisite is a healthy work-life balance, which is less about hours worked than about the culture, the autonomy over how we work, and the space to refine the craft we love. Vague answers about work-life balance are a bad sign.

What you are looking for is an organization — or at least an IT division — that understands the essence of software development at a fundamental level: that what we do is not _code_, but _think_. One of the biggest predictors of a team's success is the degree to which people feel safe to express ideas and contribute. The DORA research identifies _psychological safety_ as a key factor: individuals need a safe space to take risks and make mistakes without fear of recrimination.

Ours is a discipline primarily focused on _learning_. Whatever the sector, developing software is a process of continuous learning — not only that individuals grow, but that we make software to solve problems, and finding those solutions is a process of discovery, experimentation, and adjustment based on feedback. On a greenfield project you may start building without even knowing exactly what it is you are building. These are the real skills — not deep knowledge of any one technology, but the skills that let us learn new things.

Do your own background checks, too. The tech industry is a small world; use your network. Glassdoor is a good barometer, but look for _patterns_ rather than individual reviews — be wary of companies with lots of 5-star and lots of 1-star reviews; most companies are, by definition, average, and the most honest picture tends to be lots of 3- and 4-star reviews.

Green flags in a company:

* a strong culture of quality, with tech debt actively managed;
* pair programming, or robust QA, is valued;
* self-organizing, cross-functional teams;
* Extreme Programming practices on by default;
* room for mentoring and personal growth;
* flexible working hours and a real commitment to work-life balance;
* shift-left as much as possible.

Flexibility and work-life balance are not just perks; they are essential for sustained satisfaction and productivity. Ultimately we are assessing the _quality_ of the software systems, because that has the greatest impact on the developer experience — on avoiding burnout, working sustainably, and being able to build a long-term career.

== Making a decision

Gather all the data — Glassdoor, Trustpilot, Companies House — and then go with your gut. The few times in my career I have ignored my gut, it has been a disaster. If you don't get a good vibe from the people interviewing you, ask whether you really want to work with them: organizations attract and retain similar types of people, so the interviewer is probably representative. Do you think you can learn from them, work with them, and have fun doing so?

Remote and flexible working are not always possible — there can be legitimate reasons, such as the nature of the work or high security. If you have reservations but are willing to roll the dice, be honest and share them. And wait at least 24 hours before accepting an offer; pressure to accept quickly is a red flag.
